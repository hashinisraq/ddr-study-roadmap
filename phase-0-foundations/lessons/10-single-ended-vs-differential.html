Buses, Single-ended vs Differential Signaling

How a wire carries a bit at high speed - and why DDR's clock and strobes use pairs of wires instead of one.

Buses: shared vs point-to-point

A bus is a set of wires carrying signals between the controller and the DRAM. Some wires fan out to many chips (shared); the fastest links are point-to-point (one driver, one receiver), which is cleaner at high speed. This tension - load vs. speed - is why server modules add buffer chips (Phase 1).

Single-ended: one wire against a reference

A single-ended signal is one wire whose voltage is compared against a fixed reference (Vref): above Vref = 1, below = 0. Simple and cheap - but any noise on the wire shifts its voltage and can flip the decision.

Differential: a pair that cancels noise

A differential signal uses two wires carrying opposite versions of the same bit. The receiver looks only at the difference between them. Noise tends to hit both wires equally, so it cancels out in the difference - giving far better immunity at high speed.

Where you will see this in DDR DDR runs its clock (CK_t / CK_c) and its data strobes (DQS_t / DQS_c) as differential pairs, because these must stay rock-solid at gigahertz speeds. The data (DQ) lines are single-ended against a Vref - which is why DDR needs Vref training (Phase 1) to place that threshold perfectly.
