Phase 0 · Lesson 5

Inside a Chip: Banks and Bank Groups

You've now seen a single cell (Lesson 2) and how a whole row is sensed into the row buffer (Lesson 4). Time to zoom out one level and see how a chip is organized.

A chip is divided into "banks"

A memory chip is not one single blob of storage. Inside, it is divided into separate sections called banks. Each bank can be working on its own task at the same time as the others.

A bank is simply a full grid of cells with its own sense amplifiers and row buffer - in other words, one of the arrays you met in Lesson 4. A chip just holds many of them.

Why split it up? For speed through parallelism. Reaching into memory takes a little while; if there were only one bank, every request would have to wait in a single line. With many banks, while one bank is busy fetching, another can already be serving the next request.

Banks are organized into "bank groups"

DDR4 and DDR5 take this one step further: banks are gathered into bank groups. This extra level lets the memory switch between different groups especially quickly. (Exactly why that is faster is a DDR4 detail we'll cover in Phase 1 - for now, just know the two levels exist: bank groups, and banks inside them.)

Putting the whole picture together

You now have the full "nesting," from biggest to smallest:

LevelIn plain words
Stick (DIMM)the whole module you plug in
Ranka team of chips that together make 64 bits
Chipone of the black memory packages
Bank group → Bankindependent sections inside a chip (for parallelism)
Cellwhere a single bit actually lives (Lessons 2-4)
Analogy A chip is a supermarket; banks are its many checkout counters. One counter alone makes everyone queue; many counters serve lots of shoppers at once. Bank groups are like zones of counters that are quick to hop between.
