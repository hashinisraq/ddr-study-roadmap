Phase 0 · Lesson 5

DRAM Cell Structure: Capacitor + Access Transistor

The smallest piece of DRAM - one bit - is just two components. Get this picture right and everything else in DDR builds on it.

The one-transistor, one-capacitor cell

A single DRAM bit is stored in a 1T1C cell: one capacitor that holds the bit as an electric charge, and one transistor that acts as a switch connecting that capacitor to the outside world.

The capacitor = the bit

The capacitor stores the value as charge: charged = 1, empty = 0. It is microscopic, because billions of them are packed onto one chip.

The transistor = the switch (the "access" device)

On its own the capacitor is sealed off. The transistor connects it to the bit line only when the word line turns the transistor on. So:

  • Word line HIGH → transistor conducts → capacitor is connected to the bit line → you can read or write the bit.
  • Word line LOW → transistor off → capacitor is isolated → the bit just sits there (slowly leaking - that is the next lesson).
Analogy The capacitor is a tiny bucket of water (full = 1, empty = 0). The transistor is the tap. The word line is the hand that opens the tap; the bit line is the pipe you fill from or drain into.

Why two wires: word line and bit line

Every cell sits at the crossing of one word line and one bit line. That is deliberate: it lets you address any single cell out of billions with just a row wire and a column wire.

Why this matters Because a word line activates an entire row of cells together, DRAM never works on a single bit in isolation - it opens a whole row at a time. That single fact drives the ACTIVATE command, the row buffer, and most DDR timings you will meet in Phase 1.
