SDRAM Clocking & "Double Data Rate"

What the "S" and the "DDR" actually mean - synchronous operation, a command bus, and transferring data on both clock edges.

Synchronous: everything marches to a clock

SDRAM = Synchronous DRAM. A shared clock signal ticks continuously, and the memory captures commands and data on the clock's edges. "Synchronous" just means in step with the clock, which makes timing predictable: every action happens on a known tick.

The command/address bus

The controller tells the DRAM what to do over a command/address bus - a set of control wires whose pattern, sampled on a clock edge, encodes a command (ACTIVATE, READ, WRITE, PRECHARGE, REFRESH…). The data bus (the DQ lines) is separate and carries the actual bits in and out.

Two buses, two jobs Commands/addresses travel on one bus; data travels on another (the DQ lines, with their strobe DQS). Keeping them separate lets the chip accept a new command while data from a previous one is still flowing.

Double Data Rate: two transfers per clock tick

Plain SDRAM moved one chunk of data per clock cycle (on the rising edge). DDR moves data on both the rising and the falling edge - two transfers per cycle, doubling data throughput without doubling the clock.

This is why data rates are quoted as MT/s (mega-transfers per second), roughly double the clock in MHz - e.g. a 1600 MHz clock gives 3200 MT/s (DDR4-3200).
