Sense Amplifiers & the Row Buffer

How the chip turns a cell's near-invisible charge into a solid 0 or 1 - and why a whole "open row" sits ready for fast access.

The signal is tiny - enter the sense amplifier

When a row opens, each cell dumps its small charge onto a long bit line that already holds its own charge. The result is a minuscule voltage nudge: a stored 1 pushes the bit line up a hair, a 0 pulls it down a hair. Far too weak to use directly.

At the end of every bit line sits a sense amplifier - a circuit that detects that tiny nudge and slams it to a full, clean 0 or 1. In doing so it also writes the value back into the cell (the restore step from Lesson 2).

All the sense amps together = the row buffer

There is one sense amplifier per bit line, so when you activate a row, the entire row is sensed and latched at once. That latched row is the row buffer (also called the "open row" or "open page").

Why the row buffer makes DRAM fast Once a row is in the buffer, reading any column from it is quick - the data is already sensed and waiting. Reading many columns from one open row (a "row hit") is the fastest thing DRAM does. Having to open a different row first (a "row miss/conflict") is the slow path. Keeping useful rows open is the core of DRAM performance.
Analogy Opening a row is like opening a book to one page and laying it flat (the row buffer). Reading columns is glancing at words on that open page - instant. Switching pages means closing the book and opening another - slow.
