Why DRAM Needs Refresh: Leakage & Destructive Reads

The capacitor from Lesson 1 has two inconvenient habits: it leaks, and reading it wipes it. Both are why DRAM behaves the way it does.

Problem 1: the charge leaks away

The storage capacitor is tiny and not perfectly sealed, so its charge drains over a few milliseconds. Left alone, every stored 1 would fade to 0 and the data would rot.

Refresh is the fix: the chip periodically reads each row and writes it straight back, "topping up the buckets." In DDR4 every cell must be refreshed about every 64 ms.

Why "Dynamic" RAM The "D" in DRAM means dynamic - it forgets unless constantly reminded. (Contrast SRAM in CPU caches, which holds its value as long as it has power, but costs far more area per bit.) Refresh is not a bug; it is the price of DRAM's density and low cost.

Problem 2: reading destroys the value

When the transistor opens, the capacitor's tiny charge spills onto the long bit line and mixes with the bit line's own charge. The capacitor is left in an in-between state - the original value has been destroyed by the act of reading it.

So the hardware always re-writes the value back immediately after sensing it. A "read" is really open → sense → restore.

Why this matters later Because every read must finish restoring the row, DRAM has a minimum time a row must stay open (you will meet it as tRAS in Phase 1). Refresh and the restore step are the physical reasons behind several DDR timing rules.
